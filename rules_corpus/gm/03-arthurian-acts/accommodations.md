Travelling knights stay at **any castle, manor, monastery, or settlement** they come across. **Hospitality is an honored tradition**: the custom is to help any traveler appropriate to their status.

Most people travel very little and are **starved for news and gossip**, so non-threatening strangers are welcome — and more welcome still if they are entertaining.

> **If an enemy comes to the door**, the porter simply **stalls**, perhaps trading bitter or insulting words with the travelers, while the household's knights **take up arms and prepare to rush out and capture them**.

## By type of lodging

- **Monasteries** welcome all guests. Grooms attend the horses; guests are given a place to clean up and rest. **Separate quarters by social rank** keep nobility apart from commoners; higher nobility and **Round Table Knights may be offered the abbot's own quarters**. Commoners get austere monkish fare, while **knights and nobles eat richer victuals at the abbot's table**.
- **Peasant dwellings**, where nothing better exists. Commoners are ==usually reluctant to admit powerful strangers== and may recommend someone in town who is "less suspicious". An agreeable host tentatively accepts, then complains he is poor with nothing to spare and hints at compensation. **They dicker until a price is agreed — nothing is guaranteed except what both parties agree.**
- **Public inns do not exist yet.** They appear only in the **late Tournament Period**, first in cities and market towns, and are initially poor: no private accommodation, no menu, no food beyond common peasant fare, and everyone housed in **one large common room with a single fireplace**.
- **Camping.** If nothing can be found, knights sleep on the cold, hard ground — those travelling with **fine pavilions** find it more tolerable.
