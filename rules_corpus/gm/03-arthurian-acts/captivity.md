==**Surrendering to an enemy after being reduced to half Hit Points or less is never a dishonorable act**==, and is often the wisest course for survival. Being captured is neither shameful nor the end of a career — capture at the hands of a scheming sorceress or a robber-knight is a classic feature of the genre, and both Arthur and Lancelot are taken several times in the stories.

Conversely, **extending the offer of capture to a defeated enemy is a chivalrous act**, and the promise of ransom motivates most knights to prefer capture to killing. **Capturing enemies is potentially lucrative.**

## Capture and the Oath of Parole

Typical capture happens **during a battle**. Captured knights are **disarmed and herded together under guard**; once the fighting is over, the captives must swear the **Oath of Parole** — an oath of submission guaranteeing they will **not attempt escape or act treacherously** toward their captor.

In exchange for the oath, **the captor must supply shelter and food appropriate to the captive's rank**. ==To do otherwise deserves a **Selfish** check.==

- Depending on the captive's trustworthiness, the captor **may return the captive's sword, armor and horses** as a token of honor, earning a **Generous** check.
- This is honorable but **neither necessary nor often prudent** — the captive's arms and horses **belong to the captor by right of conquest**. ==A Player-knight does **not** take a Selfish check for keeping his captive's gear.==

## Raising the ransom

The captor allows the captive to **send word to his liege and family**, who then collect the funds — some from the peasants of the knight's holding, some from his family, some from his liege.

**What the liege contributes signals how much the captive is valued.** A favorite is assured of release, his lord paying the full amount if need be; **a troublesome or poor knight may get only a couple of libra.** A family has a duty to secure release and will pay whatever it must. A vassal knight can simply squeeze his peasants for the sum.

**Money rarely changes hands.** Ransoms are more often paid in **horses, fine cloth and expensive household wares** — tapestries, utensils, furniture — borne in carts to the captor's home. Raising the funds may take a long time; **the captive must be freed when payment is made.**

**Release on oath.** A captive may be **freed to collect his own ransom**, swearing an oath to return within a set time — ==**typically a year and a day**== — with or without the money.

## If the ransom is never paid

The captive **must serve as a loyal follower of his captor**, in borrowed armor, weapons and horse. The captor sets the length of service, but it must be enough to discharge the debt: **calculate the years required by comparing the difference in the Standard of Living at which he is kept.** A knight in such captivity would be wise to **seek loot and captives of his own** to hasten repayment.

## Scenario hooks

- The captive knight is released to collect his own ransom — **and does not return.**
- The captive **behaves treacherously while under the Oath of Parole.**
- **Nobody offers to pay** the captive's ransom, and the captor is stuck with him.
- The captor **demands an unusually large ransom**, far more than the captive is worth.
